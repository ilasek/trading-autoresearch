## Mechanism

**The object.** You searched `l` candidate models and kept the best. The right null is not "this
model is no better than the benchmark" but **"the best of the `l` is no better than the benchmark"**:

    H₀ :  max over k = 1…l  of  E[f*_k]  ≤  0

where `f_k` is candidate `k`'s performance *relative to a benchmark* (mean return difference,
negative MSE difference, directional accuracy difference, a penalised predictive log-likelihood —
White's framework is agnostic). Testing the selected model as though it were the only one ever
considered gives what White calls the **naive p-value**, and the whole paper is about how much that
naive p-value lies.

**Why a bound is not good enough.** `H₀` is an intersection of `l` one-sided hypotheses, so
Bonferroni and its refinements apply — and White's stated goal is a method that *does not* rely on
them, because they ignore dependence and, quoting Hand, "were not designed for the sheer numbers of
candidate patterns generated by data mining". The dependence is the whole point: candidates built
from the same data by varying a knob are enormously correlated, and a method that prices `l`
independent looks over-penalises them.

**The distribution to simulate.** Given asymptotic normality of the performance vector, the
max-statistic converges:

    max_k √n ( f̄_k − E[f*_k] )  ⇒  max_k Z_k,     Z ~ N(0, V)

with `V` the full `l × l` covariance of the candidates' performance. The null is enforced at its
**least favourable configuration**, `E[f*_k] = 0` for every `k`, which is what makes the test valid
without knowing which candidates are truly useless — and also what makes it **conservative**. The
distribution of the max of a vector of correlated normals has no analytic form, so it is simulated.

**The one sentence from this literature that matters most to this repo**, and it is White's own
observation about how the corrected p-value evolves as candidates accumulate: it drops each time a
new best is observed ("a new tail event"), and otherwise it **creeps up** — but the creep is gradual,
and "the p-value stays flat for modest stretches, due to the relatively high correlation among the
forecasts. **This illustrates that consideration of even a large number of models need not lead to
dramatic erosion of the Reality Check p-value.**" What sets the multiple-testing penalty is not the
count of candidates but the **effective dimension** of the space they span. See *Related* for the
tension this creates with a note already in this folder.

**Sullivan–Timmermann–White's mechanism is about the universe, not the test.** Their contribution is
the argument that the correction must be computed against **the full universe from which the winner
could plausibly have been drawn** — not the set the current researcher happened to run. Their
reasoning is that snooping is a *community-level* phenomenon: rules that entered textbooks and the
financial press did so because they had already looked good, so "the financial community may
effectively have acted as such a filter", and an honest correction has to price the filter, not just
the researcher's own last afternoon. The consequence they state flatly is the binding one:

> **the data-snooping adjustment only accounts for snooping within the space spanned by the included
> rules.**

An under-specified universe under-corrects, and the under-correction is invisible.

## Construction recipe

**White's Bootstrap Reality Check**, in the recursive form he gives — which is the form to implement,
for reasons below:

1. Fix `R` (start of the prediction sample), `T`, the number of resamples `N` (he suggests 500 or
   1000), and the stationary-bootstrap smoothing parameter `q`. Generate the `N` sets of resampling
   indexes `{u_i(t)}` **once, at the outset**, with the stationary bootstrap of Politis and Romano
   (geometric block lengths with mean `1/q`; `q = 1` is the i.i.d. bootstrap, appropriate if the
   performance differences are a martingale difference sequence; smaller `q` for more serial
   dependence).
2. Compute the benchmark's per-period performance, then for each candidate `k` its relative
   performance series `f_{k,t}` and its mean `f̄_k`; and, using the **same** indexes, the `N`
   bootstrap means `f̄*_{k,i}`.
3. Accumulate:

       V_k     = max( √n · f̄_k ,  V_{k−1} )
       V*_{k,i} = max( √n · ( f̄*_{k,i} − f̄_k ) ,  V*_{k−1,i} )      i = 1 … N

   Note the **recentering** by `f̄_k` in the bootstrap leg — that is what imposes the null.
4. p-value after `k` candidates: sort the `V*_{k,i}`, find `M` with `V*_{(M)} ≤ V_k < V*_{(M+1)}`,
   and report `P_RC = 1 − M/N`. (Refinable by interpolation or a fitted tail model.)

**Three properties of this recipe that make it unusually well suited to a lab that accumulates
candidates over time**, all of them White's own points:

- **It is incremental.** Candidates enter one at a time; adding the `(k+1)`th needs only `V_k` and
  the `N` values `V*_{k,i}`. You never revisit an earlier candidate.
- **Storage is `O(l)`, not `O(l²)`.** Only two scalars per candidate are retained. The `n × l` matrix
  of per-period performances is never needed again and need not be kept or shared.
- **It is reproducible from a seed.** The only information needed to regenerate the resampling
  indexes is `R`, `T`, `q`, `N`, an agreed random number generator and its seed. White makes the
  point explicitly: this lets the Reality Check "be carried out by researchers at separate locations
  and at separate times" without sharing data.

**The naive-versus-corrected diagnostic, which is free and is the cheapest thing in this note.**
Compute the same bootstrap p-value *treating the best candidate as if it were the only one
considered*. **The difference between the naive and the Reality Check p-value is a direct estimate of
the data-mining bias.** White also gives the ordering rule that saves the work: if the naive p-value
is already large, stop — the Reality Check p-value can only be larger. Only a small naive p-value
justifies computing the corrected one.

**Sullivan–Timmermann–White's span diagnostic**, which is the second free thing here and needs no
bootstrap at all. To check whether a candidate universe spans a meaningfully larger space than a
smaller one: form the covariance matrix of the candidates' *return series*, take its eigenvalues,
and plot them in descending order as a scree diagram. A universe whose eigenvalues collapse below a
threshold after a handful of components spans little; one whose eigenvalues stay above it for
scores of components spans a lot. They apply it by sampling a few hundred rules from their large
universe (computational capacity being the reason for sampling) and comparing against a small
published set. **This is the direct measurement of "effective number of independent candidates"
that White's flat-p-value observation says is the quantity that actually matters.**

## Robustness evidence (qualitative only)

- White's asymptotics rest on West's results for predictive inference and on Politis–Romano's
  validity for means of α-mixing processes; the test is asymptotic, with Monte Carlo support
  reported in a companion paper. He notes the approximation was *better* for directional accuracy
  than for mean squared prediction error, and that results were relatively insensitive to the choice
  of `q`.
- **The magnitude of the correction is real and can flip a conclusion.** In White's own
  directional-accuracy illustration the naive p-value was small enough to look decisively
  significant while the Reality Check p-value was not significant at all — his comment is that
  "anyone relying on this would be seriously misled". In the mean-squared-error illustration the
  naive p-value was borderline and the corrected one clearly insignificant. No dated performance
  figures are recorded here; the transferable content is the *direction and the possibility of a
  reversal*, not the numbers.
- **The correction bites least when everything works.** Sullivan–Timmermann–White observe that for a
  small published rule set in which essentially every rule beat the benchmark, accounting for
  dependence was unlikely to overturn the original finding. The corollary is the useful one:
  **the correction bites hardest when a few candidates out of many look good and the rest do not** —
  which is the signature of selection.
- **Known conservativeness.** The least-favourable configuration means genuinely useless candidates
  still contribute to the null distribution, so including junk weakens the test. This is the
  acknowledged limitation and the thing Hansen (2005) is cited for addressing — **recorded here as a
  pointer only, unread; see the header.**
- Neither primary claims the method removes snooping. White's framing throughout is that it lets
  snooping "be undertaken with some degree of confidence that one will not mistake results that
  could have been generated by chance for genuinely good results".

## Implementability here

**The machinery is implementable; the honest verdict is that the two *diagnostics* are worth more to
this lab than the test itself.**

- **Against the 2026-09-14 specification curve, the Reality Check is a direct fit.** 178 variants,
  each with a full daily return series on the same window, and an obvious benchmark (the house
  default construction, or the seated champion). The recursive recipe needs one pass. The interesting
  output is the naive-versus-corrected gap, which prices the data-mining bias in the curve *in
  p-value units*, complementing the 0.079 the lab measured in Sharpe units.
- **The span diagnostic is the higher-value item and should come first, because it is cheap, it can
  fail, and it bears on something much larger than one curve.** Take the stored return series of the
  recorded trials, form their covariance, and read the eigenvalue decay. That yields an **effective
  number of independent trials** for this repo's entire history. The lab currently deflates by the
  raw trial count; White's flat-p-value observation says the raw count over-penalises a correlated
  search, and the scree is the measurement that would say by how much. Both answers are findings:
  if the eigenvalues decay fast, the repo has run far fewer independent experiments than its counter
  says and its exploration is narrower than it believes; if they decay slowly, the deflator is doing
  roughly the right thing and the raw count is defensible.
- **Sullivan–Timmermann–White's universe argument has an uncomfortable and specific application
  here, which should be stated rather than softened.** Their point is that the correction must span
  the universe the winner was plausibly drawn from, including candidates the researcher never ran
  because the community had already filtered them. Almost every mechanism this lab tests arrives
  from `research/SUMMARY.md` — i.e. from a literature that published its winners. **The relevant
  universe is therefore larger than this repo's trial count by an unknown factor, and the honest
  reading is that the repo's multiple-testing correction is a lower bound.** This is not a reason to
  stop; it is a reason not to treat a marginal deflated-Sharpe pass as decisive. It is also the
  cleanest statement yet of why `2026-08-17-mclean-pontiff-publication-decay.md` and this note are
  the same problem seen from two ends.
- **What does not transfer.** The test answers "is the best candidate better than a benchmark",
  which is *not* the repo's promotion question — the repo asks whether a challenger beats the
  incumbent by enough, and the holdout veto then asks a separate question on a separate split. The
  Reality Check does not replace either gate and must not be presented as an alternative to them.
  And its p-value is about a *search*, so it would have to be recomputed against a pre-declared
  universe; computed post hoc against whatever was run, it inherits exactly the under-specification
  Sullivan–Timmermann–White warn about.

**Pitfalls.**

- The recentering step (`f̄*_{k,i} − f̄_k`) is what imposes the null. Omitting it silently produces a
  meaningless p-value that will look reassuring.
- `q` must reflect the serial dependence of the *performance differences*, not of returns.
- The indexes must be **shared across all candidates**. Resampling each candidate independently
  destroys the cross-candidate dependence the whole method exists to price — and that dependence is
  the reason the answer differs from Bonferroni.
- **No trial, no candidate file, no holdout read.** Both diagnostics run on series the lab already
  has, on splits it is already allowed to read.
